## 1 · 0:00–0:28 · From Random Windows to Future Forecasts

Suppose we want to predict tomorrow’s Singapore exchange rate. The timeline shows the observations available to us through today. Now suppose a model has a low error on a published benchmark. Can we reproduce that score? And did the test give the model the same information we would have today?

## 2 · 0:28–1:15 · How DeLELSTM makes a forecast

Eight exchange rates enter both branches. One branch reads them together. The other tracks each currency separately. Each currency contributes its previous state and its latest change. The model weights those sixteen vectors to reconstruct the shared state. That reconstruction predicts the next rate and feeds back into the model at the following step.

Those weights give us something we can inspect. For the pollution input, the share assigned to its latest change averaged about sixty percent at the first prediction in a window and two percent at the last, across five runs.

## 3 · 1:15–1:58 · Reproducing the published winners

We started with the random split in the released code. We trained five models on three datasets, five times each, for a total of seventy-five runs. Each circle is one run. The diamond marks the mean. A smaller root mean squared error, or RMSE, means a closer forecast on that dataset.

We recovered the published winner in all three cases. DeLELSTM had the lowest mean error on electricity and exchange rates. RETAIN led on air pollution. Compare the published and replicated means for DeLELSTM. Even the largest gap, on air pollution, was under one point two percent.

## 4 · 1:58–2:35 · Which test windows counted?

Take Exchange, on the right. The test set had seven hundred and fifty-seven windows. Only six hundred and forty entered the score in the released code because the last batch was smaller than the others.

We took the fifteen saved DeLELSTM models and scored each one twice. The only thing we changed was whether we included that last batch. Error fell in fourteen of the fifteen pairs. That gave us a reference using every random test window. What dates were in those windows?

## 5 · 2:35–3:23 · Tracing the dates and the scaler

Consider two thirty-day windows starting one day apart. They share twenty-nine dates, even if a random split puts one in training and the other in testing. We traced every Exchange test window to the source data. All seven hundred and fifty-seven shared at least one day with training.

Air pollution showed another route for information to cross the split. The highest pollution reading in the training inputs was eight hundred and eighty-six. On the same scale, the full series extends to nine hundred and ninety-four. That higher number set the upper limit for scaling. So even the scaled value of a training reading depended on held-out observations.

## 6 · 3:23–4:18 · Forecasting with the data available at the time

We redesigned the evaluation around the date of a forecast. First, we kept the observations in time order. For pollution, we filled input gaps with the most recent reading already available and recorded which targets had actually been observed.

Next we set the training, validation and test boundaries. For Exchange, we dropped the first twenty-nine window starts after each boundary. We learned the scaling parameters from training data and used those same values throughout. Validation selected the model checkpoint. Then we scored it on every window in the next test period. For pollution, only measured targets counted.

## 7 · 4:18–5:00 · Checking the time boundaries

In each row, the training history gets longer and the test moves forward. We checked the source times within every split. Across the three datasets and three folds, training, validation and testing shared zero time points within a fold. We also changed the held-out inputs and checked that the scaled training values stayed identical.

For pollution, the final period had four thousand and twenty-six candidate targets. Sixty-two were missing. The score therefore measures error on the remaining observed targets.

## 8 · 5:00–6:13 · Reading the three future periods

Read across one row at a time. The first number is the complete random-test reference from slide four. Each of the next three is a mean for a different future period. We trained only DeLELSTM for this part.

For pollution, the reference was about twenty-six. The future means were about eighteen, twenty-three and eighteen. Electricity came in below its reference twice and above it in the last period. Exchange was above its reference in all three periods, with the largest error in the first.

The middle pollution result averages three successful runs. Two stopped during training. Every other cell averages five. The test periods changed along with the training history, and the historical pollution score included filled targets. These rows describe how the model performed in each period. To measure the effect of one change, we would have to test it on the same future observations.

## 9 · 6:13–6:56 · What the replication tells us

Return to tomorrow’s exchange rate. The published comparison gave us a reproducible answer about which model had the lowest average error on those benchmark windows. The three later periods tell us what error DeLELSTM actually reached when the test dates lay ahead of training.

So when someone quotes an exchange-rate forecast error, we would ask three things. Which dates did the model learn from? Which windows went into the score? And which targets were actually observed?
