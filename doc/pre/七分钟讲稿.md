## 1 · 0:00–0:30 · The forecasting question

Imagine forecasting tomorrow’s air pollution. What information would you actually have today? We’re Group Seventeen. We asked that question about DeLELSTM, a model that forecasts several kinds of time series and explains how it uses their history. We first reproduced its published evaluation. Then we tested the same model on three later periods for each dataset.

## 2 · 0:30–1:20 · What the model explains

Suppose air pollution suddenly rises. The model uses two pieces of information about each variable. One is the state it carried over from the previous step. The other is how much that state just changed. Each gets its own weight.

Follow the solid line for the pollution variable itself. After we normalize the two sets of weights, the share assigned to the latest change is about sixty percent at the first output position. By the last position, it is about two percent. The dashed lines show the five individual runs. This tells us how the model uses a variable’s history across a window.

## 3 · 1:20–2:05 · Reproducing the historical ranking

We started with the original random split so we could check whether we could reproduce the model ranking. We ran five models with five training seeds on each of three datasets. That gives seventy-five runs. Each circle is one run, and each diamond is the average. Further left means lower forecast error.

DeLELSTM has the lowest average error on electricity demand and exchange rates. For Beijing air pollution, RETAIN comes first. So the ranking depends on the dataset, even before we change how the test period is chosen.

## 4 · 2:05–2:45 · The missing last batch

The original evaluation stopped at the last complete batch. For electricity, that meant scoring only sixty-four of the ninety-two test windows.

Each line follows one saved model. We kept its weights and restored the omitted windows. Error fell in fourteen of the fifteen comparisons. The dashed line on the left shows the one increase. We use the complete random test set as our historical reference from here on.

## 5 · 2:45–3:40 · Two paths for future information

Look at the two bars. One exchange-rate window covers days one through thirty. Move its start by a day, and the next window still contains twenty-nine of the same days. When windows are randomly assigned, one can end up in training and the other in testing. We checked the dates. Every one of the seven hundred and fifty-seven exchange-rate test windows shared at least one day with training.

Scaling introduced a second problem. In the pollution training windows, the observations ranged from one to eight hundred and eighty-six. The scaler used zero to nine hundred and ninety-four. Values outside training had already influenced what the model saw.

## 6 · 3:40–4:40 · Rebuilding the evaluation in time order

Read each row from left to right. We train on the earliest observations, choose the model on the next period, and test it on the period after that. Across the three rows, training expands from fifty-five to seventy-five percent. We fit a fresh scaler using only the training inputs in each row.

For exchange rates, we drop twenty-nine window starts after each boundary. Across all nine splits, we verified that training, validation and testing share zero original time points. For air pollution, we fill an input gap using the last available reading and score only observed targets. In the final test period, that excludes sixty-two missing targets from the error calculation.

## 7 · 4:40–5:50 · What changed on future periods

Read across each row. The first number is the historical score on the complete random test set. The next three are the average errors on successive future periods. Lower is better. We trained only DeLELSTM for this comparison.

Air pollution shows the clearest downward pattern. Its historical error was about twenty-six, and the three future scores were roughly eighteen, twenty-three and eighteen. Electricity was lower in the first two periods and higher in the last. Exchange-rate error was higher in all three future periods.

The asterisk matters. Two air pollution runs in the middle period failed during training, so that cell averages three successful seeds. Overall, forty-three of forty-five runs produced scores. The protocols also use different test windows, and the old pollution scores include filled targets. Those differences limit what we can attribute to the repair itself.

## 8 · 5:50–6:30 · How fast is one forecast?

We also timed the trained model on one H100 GPU. The inputs and weights were already on the device. We used full single precision and included the calculation of the explanation coefficients in the timing.

One window took a median of seventeen to nineteen milliseconds. With batches, throughput ranged from about seventeen hundred pollution windows per second to fifty-three hundred exchange-rate windows per second. The batch sizes appear beside each bar. These timings cover the model’s forward pass.

## 9 · 6:30–7:00 · What we can conclude

We can now test on strictly later data using a scale learned from training alone. Accuracy still varied by dataset and period. To measure how much each repair changes accuracy, we would keep the future windows, observed targets and seeds fixed, then change one part of the protocol at a time. Thank you.
