# Testing DeLELSTM on future periods

## 1 · 0:00–0:25 · The question behind the score

Imagine predicting the next Singapore exchange rate. Today we know the recent rates. The next observation is still in the future. Now suppose a model reports a very low test error. Did it face that same information limit? We asked this question about DeLELSTM.

## 2 · 0:25–1:10 · How the model makes a forecast

At one step, eight rates enter both branches. The standard LSTM produces the hidden state we want to match. The tensorized LSTM supplies eight previous state vectors and eight changes. These sixteen columns form the design matrix.

Least squares fits sixteen weights for this window and this step. Multiplying the same matrix by those weights gives the reconstructed state. Follow its two arrows. A linear readout predicts the next Singapore rate, and the standard LSTM uses the reconstructed state at its next step. The model also returns the fitted weights for interpretation.

## 3 · 1:10–1:50 · Reproducing the historical ranking

How well did this model forecast in the original benchmark? We compared five models on the same random split for each dataset. With five training seeds per model and three datasets, that gave seventy-five runs.

Each diamond marks the mean root mean squared error. Further left means a closer forecast. DeLELSTM led on electricity and exchange rates. RETAIN led on air pollution. We took a closer look at the exchange-rate score.

## 4 · 1:50–2:25 · Which windows counted?

Look at the panel on the right. The Exchange test set had seven hundred and fifty-seven windows, but only six hundred and forty were scored. The final partial batch was dropped.

We restored those windows, keeping the saved weights fixed. Across all three datasets, fourteen of fifteen errors fell. We now had a complete random-test reference. How many of those exchange-rate windows contained days the model had already seen?

## 5 · 2:25–3:20 · Two ways information crossed the split

Every one did. Think of two thirty-day windows that start a day apart. They share twenty-nine days, yet random assignment can put one in training and the other in testing. We checked the source positions. Every Exchange test window shared at least one day with training.

Pollution exposed a second problem. Its training inputs ranged from one to eight hundred and eighty-six. The scaler used zero and nine hundred and ninety-four as its endpoints. A reading in the training set was therefore being transformed using information from outside that set. Repairing the evaluation meant changing both the split and the scaler.

## 6 · 3:20–4:20 · Our data and evaluation pipeline

Start on the left. We arrange the windows in source order. For pollution we fill an input gap from the last available reading and keep a record of which target values were actually observed. We then split by time and remove exchange-rate windows that would overlap across a boundary.

The training box is where we fit the minimum and maximum of each input. The lower arrow shows those same values going into validation and testing. We learn model weights on training data, choose the checkpoint with the lowest validation error, and use those weights to score the following period. All test windows are included. A missing pollution target contributes nothing to the score.

## 7 · 4:20–5:05 · Checking that the repair worked

Each row moves the forecast further forward. The training portion grows from fifty-five to seventy-five percent of the ordered window starts. We checked the underlying time points within every split. Training, validation and testing shared zero original time points in all nine cases. We also changed held-out inputs and verified that the scaled training inputs stayed identical.

There is a second check for pollution. In the last test period, sixty-two of the four thousand and twenty-six targets were missing. Only the observed targets entered the error calculation.

## 8 · 5:05–6:20 · Reading the future-period results

Read across each row. The first number is the full random-test reference from earlier. The next three are the mean errors on successive future periods. For each period and seed we trained the same DeLELSTM architecture from scratch.

Air pollution went from a historical error of about twenty-six to roughly eighteen, twenty-three and eighteen, all lower. Electricity was below its reference in the first two periods and above it in the last. Exchange had higher error in every future period.

The asterisk marks an incomplete group. Two pollution runs in the middle period failed during training. That cell averages three successful seeds. Every other mean uses five. In total, forty-three of forty-five runs produced scores. The test dates changed along with the protocol, and the historical pollution score also included filled targets.

## 9 · 6:20–7:00 · Returning to the exchange-rate forecast

Did that low exchange-rate error carry over? The lowest future mean was about two point three times the full random-test mean. The highest was about eleven point four times as high.

We can now measure error on observations strictly later than training. To find out how much each repair changes accuracy, we would keep the future windows, observed targets and seeds fixed, then change one part of the protocol at a time. Thanks for listening.
